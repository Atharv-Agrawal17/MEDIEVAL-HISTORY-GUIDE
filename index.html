UNIT 1 — RULING

1.1 The Norman invasion and the Battle of Hastings

Why did people invade England?

In 1066, England became the target of competing claims to the throne. Edward the Confessor died without a surviving child, creating a succession crisis.

Harold Godwinson was crowned king, but William, Duke of Normandy, claimed that he had a right to the throne. Harald Hardrada, King of Norway, also invaded England.

The events of 1066

January: Edward the Confessor dies. Harold Godwinson becomes king.
September: Harald Hardrada invades northern England.
25 September: Harold defeats Hardrada at the Battle of Stamford Bridge.
28 September: William's Norman army lands in southern England.
14 October: Harold and William fight at the Battle of Hastings.
Christmas Day: William is crowned King of England at Westminster Abbey.

Why did William win?

  • Military tactics: Norman forces used cavalry, archers and infantry.
  • Harold's exhaustion: His army had marched south after fighting in the north.
  • Battlefield tactics: Norman feigned retreats may have drawn some English soldiers away from their defensive formation.
  • Leadership: William maintained control of his army during a difficult battle.
  • Harold's death: Harold was killed during the battle, contributing to the collapse of English resistance.
The famous image of Harold being struck in the eye by an arrow appears in the Bayeux Tapestry's interpretation of events. Historians cannot establish every detail of his death with certainty.

Consequences of the Norman Conquest

  • William became king and established Norman rule.
  • Many English landowners were replaced by Norman lords.
  • Castles were constructed to strengthen military control.
  • Norman French influenced English vocabulary and government.
  • Norman architecture transformed churches and castles.
  • The king gained greater control over landholding and government.
Exam connection: Explain how the succession crisis, military tactics and Harold's death contributed to William's victory.
1.2 How William the Conqueror controlled England

1. Castles

William built castles to protect strategic locations, house soldiers, intimidate opponents and demonstrate Norman authority.

Early Norman castles often used the motte-and-bailey design: a raised earth mound, a fortified tower and an enclosed courtyard. Later stone castles were more durable.

2. Feudalism

The feudal system organised landholding around obligations of loyalty and service.

  • The king granted land to powerful tenants-in-chief.
  • Tenants-in-chief provided loyalty, military support and other obligations.
  • Sub-tenants held land under greater lords.
  • Peasants worked the land and owed rents or services.

The system was not identical everywhere, and obligations varied between estates.

3. Domesday Book (1086)

William ordered a major survey of landholding and resources. Royal commissioners gathered information about estates, owners, values, resources and taxable wealth.

The survey helped the Crown understand who controlled land and what revenue could be raised. It also recorded the major changes in land ownership following the Conquest.

4. The oath of loyalty

At the 1086 gathering at Old Sarum, important landholders swore loyalty to William. This reinforced the king's claim to be the ultimate authority over landholding.

5. The Church

Norman bishops and abbots replaced many English church leaders. New churches and monasteries were built, often using Romanesque architecture.

Remember: Castles provided military control, feudal relationships organised land and service, and Domesday Book strengthened royal administration.
1.3 Thomas Becket, Henry II and the power of the Church

Who was Thomas Becket?

Thomas Becket was a close associate of Henry II and served as the king's Chancellor. In 1162, Henry appointed him Archbishop of Canterbury.

Why did they disagree?

Henry II wanted to strengthen royal authority and bring some disputes involving clergy under royal legal control. Becket defended the Church's independence and the rights of ecclesiastical courts.

The key events

1162: Becket becomes Archbishop of Canterbury.
1164: The Constitutions of Clarendon attempt to define royal authority over the Church.
1165: Becket goes into exile in France.
1170: Becket returns to England and the dispute resumes.
29 December 1170: Four knights from Henry's household murder Becket in Canterbury Cathedral.
1173: Becket is canonised by the Pope.
1174: Henry performs penance at Canterbury.

Why was Becket murdered?

The knights acted after hearing Henry's angry criticism of Becket. Their exact intentions and the degree of Henry's responsibility remain subjects of historical discussion. The knights killed Becket inside the cathedral.

Consequences

  • Becket became a martyr in the eyes of many Christians.
  • Canterbury became a major pilgrimage destination.
  • Henry's reputation suffered greatly.
  • Henry performed public penance in 1174.
  • The event demonstrated the importance of religious authority.
Key concept: Medieval kings and Church leaders could compete for authority because religious institutions possessed legal, social and spiritual influence.
1.7 The Peasants' Revolt of 1381

Background

The Black Death reduced England's population and created a shortage of workers. Many surviving labourers sought better wages and conditions.

The government attempted to restrict wages through measures such as the Ordinance of Labourers (1349) and Statute of Labourers (1351).

Causes of the revolt

  • Poll taxes: Repeated taxes placed pressure on ordinary people.
  • Labour restrictions: Workers resented limits on wages and movement.
  • Social inequality: Many peasants objected to feudal obligations.
  • Local grievances: Royal officials and landlords were accused of unfair treatment.

Important people

Wat Tyler: A prominent leader of the rebels.

John Ball: A preacher associated with messages about equality and social justice.

Richard II: The young king who met the rebels.

What happened?

Rebellion begins in Essex and Kent in June 1381.
Rebels march towards London and enter the city.
Government buildings and records are attacked.
Richard II meets the rebels and makes promises.
Wat Tyler is killed during a meeting at Smithfield.
The revolt is suppressed and royal authority is restored.

Consequences

  • The immediate rebellion failed.
  • Many rebels were punished.
  • The poll tax system was politically discredited.
  • Authorities became more cautious about imposing similar taxes.
  • Serfdom continued to decline over time, but the revolt did not immediately abolish it.
Do not write that the revolt immediately ended feudalism. Its direct political aims were not achieved.

UNIT 2 — LIVING AND WORKING

2.1 How did people live in England around 1000?

Village life

Most people lived in rural communities and depended on agriculture. Villages were commonly organised around a manor, a church, farmland and shared resources.

Homes

  • Many ordinary homes were built from timber, wattle and daub, with thatched roofs.
  • Rooms were often smoky because cooking and heating used open fires.
  • Furniture and possessions were limited for poorer households.
  • Wealthier families could afford larger buildings and more goods.

Farming

People grew crops such as wheat, barley and oats and kept animals including cattle, sheep and pigs. The agricultural year shaped work, food and festivals.

Social structure

  • King: Ruled the kingdom.
  • Nobles and thegns: Held land and provided military or political service.
  • Church officials: Led religious life and often managed land.
  • Free peasants: Farmed land under different arrangements.
  • Unfree peasants: Had restrictions and obligations tied to their status.

Religion and community

Christianity shaped the calendar, festivals, education, burial customs and ideas about morality. Churches were important community centres.

Key idea: Medieval life was shaped by agriculture, local communities, social status and religion.
2.5 The Black Death

What was it?

The Black Death was a devastating plague pandemic that reached England in 1348 and spread widely during 1348–49. It was caused by the bacterium Yersinia pestis.

How did it spread?

The disease moved along trade and travel routes. Fleas associated with rodents were important in transmission, although the precise contribution of different routes varied by place and remains a subject of research.

Symptoms and impact

  • Fever and severe illness.
  • Painful swollen lymph nodes in bubonic cases.
  • Some patients developed serious respiratory or bloodstream infection.
  • Many people died, and communities struggled to care for the sick.

How many people died?

Estimates vary, but English Heritage describes the loss as approximately one-third to one-half of England's population. Exact figures are uncertain because medieval population records were incomplete.

Consequences

  • Severe population decline.
  • Labour shortages in farming and other occupations.
  • Some surviving workers gained bargaining power.
  • Wages and prices experienced pressure and change.
  • Some villages declined or were abandoned.
  • Religious responses included prayer, processions and charitable care.
  • Repeated outbreaks continued in later centuries.

Why was it historically important?

The Black Death changed the balance between landowners and workers. It weakened existing economic arrangements and contributed to long-term social change.

Cause → Event → Consequence: Trade connections helped disease spread → the plague caused mass mortality → labour became scarce and social relationships changed.
2.6 What was life like for women in the Middle Ages?

Women's experiences were different

A woman's life depended heavily on wealth, family, marital status, location and occupation. Noblewomen, townswomen, peasant women and religious women could have very different responsibilities.

Peasant women

  • Worked in agriculture and helped with harvests.
  • Prepared food, made clothing and managed household tasks.
  • Raised children and cared for family members.
  • Could undertake paid work or sell goods locally.

Townswomen

Women participated in trade, craft production, food selling, domestic service and family businesses. Some worked as independent traders or craftspeople.

Noblewomen

Noblewomen could manage estates, supervise servants, arrange household finances and participate in political or diplomatic relationships.

Marriage and law

Medieval English law treated women differently according to marital status. A married woman's legal identity and property rights were often closely tied to her husband. Widows could sometimes manage property and business more independently.

Religion and education

Some women entered convents and became nuns. A limited number had access to advanced literacy and scholarship. Christine de Pizan, writing in the late 14th and early 15th centuries, became known for her literary works and arguments about women's education.

Historical sources often preserve more information about wealthy women than ordinary women. This affects what historians can confidently reconstruct.
2.7 How did the printing press change the world?

Before printing

Books were commonly copied by hand. Producing a manuscript required considerable time and skilled labour, so books were expensive and relatively scarce.

Movable type

Movable-type printing allowed individual letters and characters to be arranged, inked and pressed onto paper repeatedly. This made producing multiple copies more efficient than copying every page by hand.

William Caxton

William Caxton was an English merchant, diplomat and printer. He established the first printing press in England at Westminster in 1476, after learning printing on the continent.

How printing changed society

  • Books could be produced in larger numbers.
  • Printing helped lower the cost of many texts over time.
  • Ideas and information circulated more widely.
  • English-language literature became more accessible.
  • Religious, educational, scientific and practical texts spread.
  • Printing supported the growth of literacy, although access remained unequal.

Limitations

Early printed books were still costly for many people. Literacy was uneven, and printing did not instantly make education universal.

Important distinction: Gutenberg developed influential movable-type printing in Europe; Caxton introduced printing to England.
2.8 Poverty and the Poor Laws

Why were people poor?

  • Harvest failures and food shortages.
  • Illness, disability or loss of a household wage-earner.
  • Low wages and insecure employment.
  • Population growth and pressure on resources.
  • War, displacement and economic disruption.

Medieval support

Religious institutions, monasteries, hospitals, almshouses, parishes and private charity provided different forms of assistance. Support was uneven and could depend on local resources and social attitudes.

Changes during the Tudor period

The Tudor government increasingly tried to regulate begging and organise relief. Authorities distinguished between people considered unable to work and those thought capable of working.

Important developments

  • 1495: Legislation targeted vagabondage and begging.
  • 1530s: The dissolution of monasteries changed an important source of charitable support.
  • 1572: A major statute introduced compulsory local poor rates and measures against vagrancy.
  • 1598: Further legislation developed parish responsibilities.
  • 1601: The Elizabethan Poor Law established a more systematic parish-based framework.

How did poor relief work?

Parishes collected local rates and appointed officials to administer relief. Assistance could include money, food, clothing, support for children or arrangements for employment.

The 1601 Poor Law belongs to the end of the Tudor period. It should not be confused with the later Victorian workhouse system or the 1834 Poor Law Amendment Act.
2.9 How did people enjoy themselves in Tudor times?

Entertainment depended on social class

Wealth, location and occupation influenced what people could afford to do. Rural workers, merchants, nobles and royalty experienced leisure differently.

Popular activities

  • Music, dancing and singing.
  • Seasonal fairs and market celebrations.
  • Religious festivals and community gatherings.
  • Storytelling and popular plays.
  • Games involving skill, strength or chance.
  • Feasts and celebrations for important occasions.

Rich and poor

Wealthy households could host elaborate feasts, masques, musicians and performances. Ordinary people often enjoyed local festivals, dancing, games and community celebrations.

Theatre

During the later Tudor period, professional theatre developed rapidly. Public playhouses became important centres of entertainment, particularly in London.

Religion and leisure

Religious festivals shaped the calendar. Reformation changes altered some traditional celebrations and religious practices.

Historical thinking: Avoid assuming that every person in Tudor England enjoyed the same activities. Social class and local customs mattered.

UNIT 3 — MOVING AND TRAVELLING

3.1 The impact of Arab culture on the medieval world

Travel and trade

The medieval Islamic world connected regions across North Africa, the Middle East, parts of Europe and Asia. Merchants travelled by land and sea, exchanging goods, knowledge and ideas.

Trade goods

  • Spices and aromatics.
  • Textiles and silk.
  • Paper, books and manufactured goods.
  • Metals, ceramics and glassware.
  • Agricultural products.

Important trade routes

The Mediterranean connected ports in Europe, North Africa and the Middle East. Caravan routes crossed deserts and linked cities and trading centres. Indian Ocean routes connected East Africa, Arabia, India and Southeast Asia.

Science and medicine

Scholars working in Arabic and other languages studied, translated, criticised and developed knowledge inherited from Greek, Persian, Indian and other traditions.

Important scholars

  • Al-Khwarizmi: Important contributions to algebra and mathematical methods.
  • Ibn Sina (Avicenna): Wrote the influential medical work known as the Canon of Medicine.
  • Al-Razi: Physician and medical writer whose works influenced later medicine.
  • Ibn al-Haytham: Made important contributions to optics and the study of vision.

Medicine

Medical scholars organised earlier knowledge, developed medical encyclopedias and investigated treatments. Hospitals in several Islamic cities served as centres for patient care and medical learning.

Mathematics and astronomy

Scholars developed algebraic methods, mathematical tables and astronomical instruments. The astrolabe was used to make observations and solve practical problems involving the sky.

How did knowledge travel?

  • Merchants carried books and ideas between regions.
  • Scholars translated works between languages.
  • Libraries and learning centres preserved manuscripts.
  • Travel and cultural contact encouraged exchange.
  • Texts were translated into Latin and other languages, influencing later European scholarship.
Important: Scientific knowledge developed through many cultures. Medieval Islamic scholarship both preserved earlier learning and produced original contributions.
3.2 Medieval travel and connections

Why did people travel?

  • Trade and business.
  • Religious pilgrimage.
  • Diplomacy and government service.
  • Education and scholarship.
  • Military campaigns.
  • Migration and family connections.

Travel in England

People travelled on foot, by horse, in carts and by boat. Road conditions varied, and journeys could be slow, expensive and uncomfortable.

Risks

  • Bad weather and difficult roads.
  • Robbery and theft.
  • Illness and injury.
  • River crossings and unsafe routes.
  • Unfamiliar languages and local customs.

How travel connected societies

Travellers moved goods, stories, religious ideas, technologies and knowledge. Ports, markets, fairs and pilgrimage sites became meeting points for people from different regions.
